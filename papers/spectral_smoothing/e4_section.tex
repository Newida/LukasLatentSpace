We compare the estimators at $n=10$, where every quantity can be computed exactly. The \emph{clustered} target is an equal mixture of four product distributions around random centres, each bit flipped with probability $0.1$. This target is smooth in Hamming distance. The \emph{checksum} target is the same mixture conditioned on even parity, which is a hard constraint that Hamming smoothing violates. For each target we draw $m\in\{30,100,300,1000\}$ samples over $10$ seeds.

The estimators are:
\begin{itemize}
\item the empirical distribution;
\item add-$\alpha$ (uniform pseudo-counts);
\item the heat / Aitchison--Aitken kernel;
\item the Tikhonov filter~\eqref{eq:p2};
\item penalised maximum likelihood~\eqref{eq:p1};
\item the learned positive isotropic filter of \cref{sec:filters} (LOO-EM), which has no hyperparameter.
\end{itemize}
Hyperparameters are selected on a $20\%$ validation split with the quadratic score $\|q\|_2^2-\frac{2}{|V|}\sum_{x\in V}q(x)$. This proper scoring rule~\cite{gneiting2007scoring} is finite for every estimator, unlike held-out log-likelihood. We then refit on all $m$ samples. \Cref{tab:gen} and \cref{fig:gen} report the total-variation distance to the truth.

\paragraph{Smooth target.}
The spectral smoothers (heat, Tikhonov and the learned filter) beat both the empirical distribution and add-$\alpha$ at every $m$. The gain is largest at small $m$: at $m=30$, TV drops from $0.61$ to $0.42$--$0.47$. Heat and Tikhonov are statistically indistinguishable, so the exponential mixture over bandwidths neither helps nor hurts once the bandwidth is tuned. The learned filter is best at every $m$, without any hyperparameter search.

\paragraph{Penalised likelihood does not smooth where it matters.}
P1 barely improves on the empirical distribution, and it has infinite KL in every run. This is \cref{prop:support} at work. In the runs we inspected ($m=100$ and $1000$), the support of the P1 optimum stays exactly equal to the set of distinct observed strings across the whole useful range of $\mu$. P1 can therefore only \emph{reweight} the observed strings: it compresses the frequent ones and lifts the rare ones, but it cannot move mass onto unseen strings, and that is where most of the error lies. Larger $\mu$ only hurts, so validation drives $\mu\to0$ at large $m$. The cause is the likelihood term, which rewards mass on the data and gives no reward for mass elsewhere. The $\ell_2$ data term of P2 has no such effect.

\paragraph{The checksum counterexample.}
On the parity-constrained target, the Hamming smoothers lose to the empirical distribution and never recover the gap. At $m\le100$, add-$\alpha$, heat and Tikhonov put $6$--$29\%$ of their mass on invalid strings. The learned isotropic filter is the exception. It places \emph{exactly zero} mass on invalid strings and is the best estimator at every $m$ (tied with the empirical distribution at $m=1000$). Its weights show why. For one seed at $m=100$, EM returns $w_0=0.74$, $w_2=0.26$ and $w_d=0$ otherwise. All pairwise distances within the data are even, so the leave-one-out likelihood puts all weight on even shells, and ``flip exactly two bits'' preserves parity. Isotropic does not mean Hamming-local. The positive isotropic class of \cref{thm:isotropic} is rich enough to express some hard constraints, while remaining exactly samplable in $O(n)$. The price is that zero weight on some shells can leave valid strings with zero mass, so its KL is not always finite at small $m$.
