QAOA on the Hamming Cube

The quantum approximate optimization algorithm looks like a list of gates. Seen on the right stage it is a familiar numerical method: it alternates between the two natural coordinate systems of the bitstring cube, positions and Walsh frequencies, the same way a split-step Fourier solver alternates between position and momentum.

You need linear algebra and a little quantum notation (|x⟩, Pauli X and Z, the Hadamard gate). Everything else is built up here.

PART 1

The stage: bitstrings on a cube

An n-qubit basis state |x⟩ is labelled by a bitstring x ∈ {0,1}n. Put the 2n strings on the corners of an n-dimensional cube and join two corners by an edge when they differ in exactly one bit. Walking along edges, the distance between two corners is their Hamming distance. This graph is the Hamming cube Qn.

A combinatorial optimization problem is a function on the corners. For MaxCut on a graph G, bit xi says which side vertex i is on, and C(x) counts the edges whose endpoints land on different sides. The task is to find the corner where C is largest.

Moving along one edge of the cube means flipping one bit, which is what the Pauli operator Xi does to a basis state. So the adjacency matrix of the cube is a sum of bit flips:

A = X1 + X2 + ⋯ + Xnadjacency matrix of the Hamming cube

This is exactly the operator QAOA calls the mixer (usually written B). The mixer is not an arbitrary choice of gates. It is the graph structure of the search space, written in qubit notation. Everything below follows from taking that seriously.

PART 2

Fourier analysis on the cube

Under bitwise XOR the corners form a group, ℤ2n, and every group of this kind has its own Fourier transform. Its “sine waves” are the characters

χs(x) = (−1)s·x,   s ∈ {0,1}none character per frequency s

The character χs changes sign whenever you flip a bit that lies inside the support of s, and ignores all other bits. The Hamming weight |s| therefore plays the role of frequency: χ000 is constant, the weight-1 characters are square waves along one axis, and χ111 changes sign across every edge. The Fourier coefficients of a function are

f̂(s) = 2−n Σx f(x) χs(x)the Walsh–Hadamard transform

On qubits this transform is a gate you already know: H⊗n, the Hadamard on every qubit, up to the normalization 2−n/2. Its matrix entries are 2−n/2 χs(x).

Now the key identity. Flipping bit i multiplies χs by (−1)si. Summing over all bits, the n − |s| bits outside the support contribute +1 each and the |s| bits inside contribute −1 each:

A χs = (n − 2|s|) χs,    equivalently  H⊗n A H⊗n = Z1 + ⋯ + Zn

The Fourier transform diagonalizes the cube, exactly as ordinary Fourier modes diagonalize the Laplacian on a line. In gate language it is the familiar HXH = Z, applied to every qubit at once.

Cost functions have sparse spectra

MaxCut is especially clean in this basis. Write ei for the string with a single 1 at position i. Edge (i, j) is cut when xi ≠ xj, which is (1 − χei⊕ej(x))/2. Summing over edges:

C(x) = |E|/2 − ½ Σ(i,j)∈E χei⊕ej(x)

The MaxCut objective has Fourier weight only at frequency 0 and at weight-2 frequencies, one per graph edge. Many optimization problems are low-degree like this: their spectra are sparse and concentrated at small |s|. That sparsity is what makes QAOA’s operators local and implementable as short circuits.

PART 3

Classical diffusion: heat flow on the cube

Start with something classical. Let a walker sit on a corner and let each of its bits flip independently at rate 1. Its probability distribution pt obeys the heat equation on the cube,

dp/dt = −L p,    L = n I − Agraph Laplacian of the cube

Because A is diagonal in the Fourier basis, so is L, with eigenvalue 2|s|. Each Fourier mode just decays:

p̂t(s) = e−2|s|t p̂0(s)

High frequencies die first, and as t → ∞ only the constant mode survives: the uniform distribution. In position space the same evolution factorizes over bits. After time t, every bit has flipped with probability qt = (1 − e−2t)/2, independently of the others. This is the noise operator of Boolean Fourier analysis, and it is the mutation step of a genetic algorithm.

PART 4

Quantum diffusion: the mixer

Replace the heat equation by the Schrödinger equation on the same graph: i dψ/dβ = A ψ. Its solution is the QAOA mixer, ψβ = e−iβA ψ0, a continuous-time quantum walk on the cube. In the Fourier basis:

ψ̂β(s) = e−iβ(n − 2|s|) ψ̂0(s)

Nothing decays. Every mode keeps its magnitude and rotates its phase at a speed set by its frequency. Classical diffusion forgets the starting point by damping fine structure; the quantum walk keeps all of it and turns it into phase.

Position space again factorizes, now over amplitudes. Since e−iβX = cos β · I − i sin β · X on each qubit, starting from |x⟩ the amplitude to reach a corner at Hamming distance k is

(cos β)n−k (−i sin β)k

Measured right away, this looks like classical bit flipping with probability sin2β. The difference is the phase (−i)k, which lets amplitudes from different paths interfere as soon as anything else acts on the state. One more difference stands out at β = π/2: every bit flips with certainty and the walk lands on the opposite corner exactly. The classical walk never puts more than 2−n probability there. Halfway, at β = π/4, each bit flips with probability exactly ½ and the measured distribution is exactly uniform. The classical walk only approaches uniform as t → ∞ (Moore and Russell, see Literature).

PART 5

The cost layer is a potential

The other QAOA ingredient is e−iγC. It is diagonal in position space: it multiplies the amplitude at corner x by e−iγC(x). In the language of the Schrödinger equation, C is a potential and the mixer is the kinetic term.

On its own the cost layer changes no probabilities. Its effect is in frequency space. QAOA starts from |+⟩⊗n, the uniform superposition, which is the single Fourier mode s = 0. Multiplying by the phase pattern e−iγC(x) spreads weight onto other frequencies, according to the spectrum of that pattern. The mixer then rotates those modes at different speeds, and when you transform back to positions the phase differences have become amplitude differences. Good angles make them add up on corners with high C.

PART 6

QAOA is a split-step Fourier method

The depth-p QAOA state is

|γ, β⟩ = e−iβpA e−iγpC ⋯ e−iβ1A e−iγ1C |+⟩⊗n

Write each mixer as e−iβA = H⊗n e−iβΣZi H⊗n. Then every layer is four steps, alternating between two bases in which each operator is just a multiplication:

e−iγC(x)Positions: phase kick from the cost
→
H⊗nTransform to Walsh frequencies
→
e−iβ(n−2|s|)Frequencies: phase rotation from the mixer
→
H⊗nTransform back to positions, repeat p times

This is the split-operator method used to solve i∂tψ = (T + V)ψ numerically: FFT to momentum space, apply the kinetic phase, FFT back, apply the potential phase. On the cube, the Walsh–Hadamard transform plays the FFT, the cube Laplacian plays −∇2, and the cost plays the potential. Three consequences follow directly:

  • With many layers and small angles, QAOA is a Trotter discretization of continuous evolution under a(t) A + b(t) C. Adiabatic optimization and quantum-walk search are special schedules of this one equation.
  • A classical simulation costs O(p n 2n) using the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform. The figure below runs exactly this in your browser.
  • At depth 1 the mixer cannot change Fourier magnitudes, only phases. Whatever frequency content the cost layer creates is all the mixer has to work with.

PART 8

Rotate time and you get a genetic algorithm

Replace each unitary by its imaginary-time counterpart. The mixer e−iβA becomes the heat kernel e−tL, which is independent bit-flip mutation. The cost layer e−iγC becomes eγC followed by normalization, which is Boltzmann selection: corners with higher C get exponentially more weight. Alternating the two is a mutation–selection process, the population form of a genetic algorithm or population annealing.

Since e−tL = e−nt etA, the alternation is a Trotterization of eτ(C + κA). Repeated, it converges to the top eigenvector of C + κA, a positive vector that concentrates on the maximizers of C as κ → 0. The classical cousin works by damping: unwanted modes shrink and disappear.

QAOA is the real-time version of the same alternation. It cannot damp anything, because every step is unitary. It can only steer phases so that amplitudes interfere constructively on good corners. That is why its angles must be tuned, why its landscape has the oscillating structure you see above, and why an advantage over the classical cousin is something to establish problem by problem rather than assume.

Literature

Grouped by the part of this tutorial they support.

The algorithm

  • E. Farhi, J. Goldstone, S. Gutmann, A Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (2014). The original paper; introduces the transverse-field mixer and the alternating ansatz.
  • S. Hadfield et al., From the QAOA to a Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz (2017). Other mixers for constrained problems, where the cube is replaced by other graphs on the feasible set.

Fourier analysis on the cube (Parts 2 and 3)

  • R. O’Donnell, Analysis of Boolean Functions. Walsh characters, the noise operator Tρ (the heat flow of Part 3 with ρ = e−2t) and low-degree spectra.

The mixer as a quantum walk on the hypercube (Part 4)

  • C. Moore, A. Russell, Quantum Walks on the Hypercube (2001). Shows the continuous-time walk is exactly uniform at a specific time, faster than the classical walk’s Θ(n log n) mixing. In the units used here that time is β = π/4.
  • J. Kempe, Quantum random walks: an introductory overview (2003), and Mixing Times in Quantum Walks on the Hypercube. Hitting times to the opposite corner and the subtleties of quantum mixing times.
  • Mixing of Quantum Walks on Generalized Hypercubes (2008). The same Fourier argument on group-circulant graphs: characters (−1)a·x diagonalize the walk.

QAOA as alternating phase kicks and quantum walks (Parts 5 and 6)

  • S. Marsh, J. B. Wang, Combinatorial optimisation via highly efficient quantum walks (2019). The quantum walk optimisation algorithm (QWOA): quality-dependent phase shifts alternated with walks on circulant graphs, which the Fourier transform diagonalizes. The direct generalization of the split-step view.
  • Analysis of the Non-variational Quantum Walk-based Optimisation Algorithm (2024). A follow-up analysis of that family.
  • Depth scaling of unstructured search via quantum approximate optimization (2024). Recovers QAOA sequences by Trotterizing a continuous-time quantum walk and bounds the depth with Trotter-error theory.
  • Guided quantum walk (2023). Steers the hypercube walk using the spectrum of the problem Hamiltonian instead of variational angles.

Landscapes and mechanisms (Part 7)

  • L. Zhou, S.-T. Wang, S. Choi, H. Pichler, M. D. Lukin, QAOA: Performance, Mechanism, and Implementation on Near-Term Devices (2018). Optimized MaxCut angles and evidence that QAOA uses non-adiabatic mechanisms. Note that its “FOURIER” heuristic is a Fourier series over the layer index of the angles, a different Fourier transform from the one on the cube.
  • Connecting the Hamiltonian structure to the QAOA energy and Fourier landscape structure (2023). Treats ⟨C⟩(γ, β) as a Fourier series in the angles, with frequencies set by the eigenvalues of the cost and mixer. This explains the periodicity in the heatmap.

I found no single reference that presents QAOA explicitly as a split-step Fourier method on the cube, or that derives the imaginary-time mutation–selection analogue of Part 8. Those framings are assembled here from the standard facts above, so check them against the sources before citing.
